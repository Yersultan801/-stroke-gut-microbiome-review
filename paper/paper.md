# Abstract
Stroke remains one of the leading causes of mortality and long-term disability worldwide. Emerging evidence suggests that the gut microbiome plays a crucial role in modulating neuroinflammation, immune responses, and metabolic pathways that influence stroke risk, severity, and recovery. Dysbiosis can contribute to endothelial dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and disruption of the gut–brain axis, potentially worsening ischemic brain injury. This review summarizes current evidence on the relationship between stroke and gut microbiome composition, explores key molecular mechanisms including microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids and trimethylamine N-oxide, and highlights potential therapeutic strategies such as probiotics, prebiotics, fecal microbiota transplantation, and dietary interventions. Understanding microbiome–stroke interactions may open new opportunities for prevention and personalized treatment in cerebrovascular disease.

# Introduction
Stroke is a major global health problem and remains a leading cause of death and disability. Despite advances in acute management and rehabilitation, stroke prevention and recovery remain challenging. In recent years, growing attention has been directed toward the gut microbiome as a modifiable factor influencing cardiovascular and neurological diseases. The gut microbiome, consisting of trillions of microorganisms, regulates immune homeostasis, metabolic processes, and inflammatory signaling pathways. Emerging studies suggest that gut dysbiosis may contribute to stroke development and influence outcomes through mechanisms involving systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and altered microbial metabolites. This review discusses the bidirectional relationship between stroke and the gut microbiome and evaluates potential clinical implications.

# The Gut–Brain Axis and Stroke
The gut–brain axis is a complex bidirectional communication network linking the gastrointestinal system and the central nervous system through neural, immune, and endocrine pathways. Microbial metabolites, inflammatory cytokines, and vagal nerve signaling can influence brain function and neuroinflammation. During stroke, acute ischemic injury can disrupt autonomic regulation and intestinal barrier integrity, leading to increased permeability and translocation of microbial products into systemic circulation. This process may amplify systemic inflammation and worsen neuronal damage.

# Gut Microbiome Dysbiosis as a Risk Factor for Stroke
Several studies have demonstrated differences in gut microbiome composition between stroke patients and healthy controls. Reduced microbial diversity and increased abundance of opportunistic pathogens have been reported in ischemic stroke cases. Dysbiosis may promote chronic inflammation, metabolic disturbances, and atherosclerosis, thereby increasing stroke risk. Certain microbial profiles are associated with hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and dyslipidemia, which are established stroke risk factors.

# Microbial Metabolites and Stroke Pathophysiology
Microbial metabolites play an essential role in stroke-related mechanisms.

## Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)
SCFAs such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate are produced by bacterial fermentation of dietary fiber. These metabolites support intestinal barrier integrity, regulate immune responses, and may exert neuroprotective effects. Reduced SCFA-producing bacteria have been observed in stroke patients, potentially contributing to increased inflammation and worse outcomes.

## Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO)
TMAO is produced from dietary nutrients such as choline and carnitine via gut microbial metabolism. Elevated plasma TMAO levels have been linked to atherosclerosis, thrombosis, and increased cardiovascular risk. Several studies suggest that high TMAO concentrations are associated with stroke incidence and poor prognosis.

## Lipopolysaccharides (LPS)
LPS, a component of Gram-negative bacterial cell walls, can enter circulation when intestinal permeability is increased. Elevated LPS levels promote systemic inflammation and may contribute to endothelial dysfunction and thrombogenesis, increasing cerebrovascular risk.

# Stroke-Induced Alterations of the Gut Microbiome
Stroke itself can significantly alter gut microbiome composition. Acute brain injury can impair gastrointestinal motility, reduce microbial diversity, and promote overgrowth of pathogenic bacteria. Stroke-induced dysbiosis can further contribute to immune dysregulation and increase susceptibility to infections such as pneumonia and sepsis, which are common complications after stroke.

# Immunological Mechanisms Linking the Gut Microbiome and Stroke
The gut microbiome influences immune cell differentiation and systemic immune responses. Gut-derived immune cells can migrate to the brain and contribute to post-stroke inflammation. Dysbiosis may promote pro-inflammatory T helper 17 cells and reduce regulatory T cells, shifting the immune balance toward neuroinflammatory damage. Additionally, gut microbiota can influence microglial activation and cytokine production, affecting neuronal survival.

# Therapeutic Strategies Targeting the Gut Microbiome
Microbiome-based interventions may represent a promising approach in stroke prevention and recovery.

## Diet and lifestyle modification
Diet strongly shapes gut microbiome composition. High-fiber diets promote SCFA-producing bacteria and may reduce inflammation. Mediterranean-style dietary patterns have been linked to reduced cardiovascular risk and improved microbial diversity.

## Probiotics and prebiotics
Probiotics may restore beneficial microbial populations, while prebiotics support growth of protective bacteria. Some experimental studies indicate probiotics can reduce infarct size and improve neurological outcomes, though clinical evidence remains limited.

## Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)
FMT involves transferring gut microbiota from healthy donors to patients. Animal models suggest FMT may improve post-stroke recovery and reduce inflammation. However, its safety and efficacy in human stroke patients require further investigation.

## Antibiotic therapy
Antibiotics can alter gut microbiota and may reduce infection risk in stroke patients. However, antibiotic-induced dysbiosis may have negative long-term effects and requires careful consideration.

# Future Directions and Research Challenges
Although evidence linking gut microbiome and stroke is increasing, several challenges remain. Most clinical studies are observational and cannot establish causality. Variability in microbiome analysis methods and patient populations complicates interpretation. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies, standardized sequencing approaches, and clinical trials evaluating microbiome-targeted therapies. Identifying specific microbial biomarkers could support personalized stroke risk prediction and individualized treatment strategies.

# Conclusion
The gut microbiome plays an important role in stroke pathophysiology through immune modulation, microbial metabolites, and gut–brain axis interactions. Dysbiosis may contribute to stroke risk and influence recovery outcomes. Microbiome-targeted interventions such as dietary changes, probiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation represent promising therapeutic opportunities. Further clinical research is needed to establish causality and develop evidence-based microbiome-based strategies for stroke prevention and rehabilitation.
